Query-budgeted screened DH

What was proved about running screened DH when the platform can only afford a limited number of oracle calls per user: every variable, every assumption, the theorems, and how each proof works.

74 new theorems project: 973 theorems, 0 open discrete and continuous priors vault note: Query Budgets in Screened DH

1The short version

The problem. Screened DH (SDH) asks an LLM oracle about pairs before showing them. Its plan says how often to use each pair, and it then queries each pair as often as that plan needs. It never asks what the queries cost. On the speed-dating data it ends up querying 96–100% of all pairs. A real platform has a budget of oracle calls per user.

The fix. Make the query probability of each pair a variable of the plan, next to how often the pair is used, and add one constraint per user: expected calls at most the budget. Running SDH on such a plan is Q-SDH.

What is proved, for discrete priors and for any priors with finite means (continuous included):

  • (a) Truthful. Swiping right exactly when a match is worth its cost stays a dominant strategy.
  • (b) Within budget. Each user's expected number of oracle calls is at most its budget.
  • (c) Every user is protected. Each user gets at least \(1/4\) of what the plan promised it with random-order selection, and \(c_K^2\) with balanced selection rules.
  • (d) Total welfare. SDH's own selection (the best capped set of the pairs it found) earns at least \(c_K^2\) of the plan's total value.
  • (e) The plan is an upper bound. No budgeted policy, even one that picks its queries adaptively after seeing earlier answers, gives users more than the best plan can.
  • (f) Any reasonable objective. For max-min, or any objective that is monotone and scales with the utilities, Q-SDH gets at least \(1/4\) (or \(c_K^2\)) of the best budgeted policy's value.
In plain words

Pay for the queries you plan, plan with the queries you can afford, and you lose at most a constant factor against anyone with the same budget, for the worst-off user as much as for the total.

3Calls and budgets

A query of pair \(e\) asks the oracle for one side's value at a time, in a fixed order: first the side less likely to accept, and the other side only if the first says yes. A "no" from the first side settles the pair, so starting with the likelier "no" saves calls.

4The plan (Q-LP)

The platform chooses two things per pair before anything is shown:

  • \(\tau_e\in[0,1]\): how often to pay to look at \(e\);
  • \(y_e(s)\in[0,\tau_e]\): how often to use \(e\) when its values turn out to be \(s\). A measurable function of \(s\).

The constraints, each with its reason:

ConstraintWhy
\(0\le y_e(s)\le\tau_e\le 1\)you can't use a pair more often than you look at it
\(y_e(s)=0\) when \(s\notin A_e\)never plan to use a pair someone would reject
\(\sum_{j}\int y_{ij}\,d\mu_{ij}\le K_i\) (and for each \(j\))expected matches within the cap
\(\sum_{e\ni\ell}\tau_e a^\ell_e\le Q_\ell\)expected calls within the budget

In Lean this is ContFeasible QI QJ y τ (continuous) and QFeasible QI QJ x τ (discrete, where the plan is \(x_{e,c}=y_e(c)\,\pi_{e,c}\) and the first row reads \(x_{e,c}\le\tau_e\pi_{e,c}\)).

How this differs from SDH's plan

SDH chooses only \(y\) (its \(x\)) and then sets \(\tau_e\) to the smallest value that supports it, \(\tau_e=\max_s y_e(s)\). The truthful-swiping note's Theorem 3(d) handles a budget by shrinking the whole plan by a factor \(\lambda\). Both are special cases of the Q-LP, so the best Q-LP plan is never worse than either (qfeasible_scaled).

Why the new freedom helps

With \(\tau_e\) free, the plan can look at a pair less often and accept more of its outcomes when it does. SDH's plan, by contrast, looks at every pair whose best outcome could beat the price of the two users' slots, and keeps only the best outcomes. Section 5 shows the trade-off on one pair.

5One pair, three plans

Ann (an initiator) and Bo (a responder), both with match cost \(1\). Ann's value for Bo is uniform on \([0,4]\); Bo values Ann at exactly \(2\). So \(s=(q,2)\), \(w_I=q-1\), \(w_J=1\), and the pair is acceptable when \(q\gt1\), which happens with probability \(3/4\). Ann accepts with probability \(3/4\) and Bo always does, so Ann is asked first: \(a^{\text{Ann}}=1\), \(a^{\text{Bo}}=3/4\).

Three plans that all use the pair with the same probability \(X=1/4\):

Plan\(\tau\)\(y(s)\)\(X\)\(G^I\)\(G^J\)valueAnn's callsBo's calls
A: always look, keep only great outcomes\(1\)\(\mathbf 1[q\gt3]\)1/45/81/47/813/4
B: look half the time, keep good ones\(1/2\)\(\tfrac12\mathbf 1[q\gt2]\)1/41/21/43/41/23/8
C: look a third of the time, keep anything acceptable\(1/3\)\(\tfrac13\mathbf 1[q\gt1]\)1/43/81/45/81/31/4

For example, plan B: \(X=\tfrac12\Pr[q\gt2]=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac14\) and \(G^I=\tfrac12\int_2^4\frac{q-1}{4}\,dq=\tfrac12\). SDH's own rule picks plan A, the most valuable, and pays a full call. If Ann's budget is \(1/2\), plan A is infeasible and the Q-LP picks B.

The same pair in the surrogate market (used in section 8)

For the proofs, a plan is summarized by three numbers per pair: \(X\), \(G^I\), \(G^J\). The surrogate pair always exists, is used with probability \(X\), and is worth \(\bar w_I=G^I/X\) to Ann and \(\bar w_J=G^J/X\) to Bo: the expected net values given that the pair is used. For plan A that is \(\bar w_I=5/2\), \(\bar w_J=1\); for plan C, \(\bar w_I=3/2\), \(\bar w_J=1\).

6The mechanism (Q-SDH)

  1. Plan. Pick a feasible \((\tau,y)\).
  2. Screen. Query each pair independently with probability \(\tau_e\), asking the less likely side first.
  3. Thin. Keep a found acceptable pair with values \(s\) with probability \(y_e(s)/\tau_e\). Such a pair is a candidate. Given its values, a pair is a candidate with probability \(\tau_e\cdot y_e(s)/\tau_e=y_e(s)\), independently of every other pair.
  4. Select, in one of two ways:
    • Two stages with rules (for per-user guarantees, as in MM-SDH). Each responder \(j\) keeps a random set of its candidates of size at most \(K_j\), drawn by its rule \(\psi_1(j)\). Each initiator \(i\) then keeps a random set of what was passed to it, of size at most \(K_i\), drawn by \(\psi_2(i)\).
    • SDH's own selection (for total welfare): the best capped set of all found acceptable pairs, by true net weight. Thinning is then only a device of the proof.
  5. Show the selected pairs as SDH does: initiations in period 1, answers in period 2.

Selection rules

A rule for a user with cap \(K\) maps the user's candidate set \(A\) to a random subset \(T\subseteq A\) with \(|T|\le K\) (IsRule). Rules see only which pairs are candidates, never their values. That keeps the mechanism swipe-local, and it is what makes the continuous case reduce to the discrete one.

A rule is \(\gamma\)-balanced for candidate probabilities \(p\) if, whenever each element is a candidate independently with probability \(p_e\), the rule keeps each \(e\) with probability at least \(\gamma\,p_e\) (IsBalanced). Balance is a promise to every element, which is what a per-user guarantee needs.

  • Random order keeps a uniformly random \(\min(K,|A|)\) of the candidates (uniformRule). It is \(1/2\)-balanced whenever the expected number of candidates is at most \(K\), which the caps guarantee.
  • \(c_K\)-balanced rules exist (exists_balanced_rule, by Sion's minimax theorem). They are not explicit, and they depend on the plan's candidate probabilities but never on values or swipes.

7Theorem Q

Fix a feasible plan \((\tau,y)\). Lean names are given as discrete version, then continuous version.

Q(a) Truthful

Each user is shown a capped set of acceptable partners chosen from oracle answers and coins, so sincere swiping (swipe right iff the match is worth its cost) is a dominant strategy against any behavior of the others.

Lean: sdh_dsic (already proved for every planned set of acceptable pairs; nothing new needed)
Q(b) Calls within budget
\[ \mathbb E[\text{calls charged to }\ell]=\sum_{e\ni\ell}\tau_e\,a^\ell_e\ \le\ Q_\ell . \]
Lean: qsdh_callsI qsdh_callsJ · contCallsI_eq contCallsJ_eq
Q(c) Every user keeps a share of its promise

If every stage-1 rule is \(\gamma_1\)-balanced and every stage-2 rule is \(\gamma_2\)-balanced,

\[ \mathbb E[u_\ell]\ \ge\ \gamma_1\gamma_2\,\mathrm{lp}_\ell(y)\qquad\text{for every user }\ell. \]

Random order gives \(\gamma_1\gamma_2=1/4\). Balanced rules give \(c_K^2\).

Lean: qsdh_quarter qsdh_cK · contUtil_ge contUtil_quarter contUtil_cK
Q(d) Total welfare with SDH's own selection
\[ \mathbb E\big[\text{weight of the best capped set of found pairs}\big]\ \ge\ c_K^2\sum_\ell \mathrm{lp}_\ell(y)=c_K^2\sum_e\int w\,y_e\,d\mu_e . \]

With no budget and SDH's own \(\tau\), this is the truthful-swiping note's Theorem 3(b), now for continuous priors as well.

Lean: qsdh_welfare · contSDH_welfare (key step rules_le_bestMatch)
Q(e) No budgeted policy beats the plan

Every budgeted screened policy \(P\) (section 8.4), adaptive or not, induces a feasible plan \((\tau^P,y^P)\) that promises every user exactly what \(P\) gives it:

\[ \mathrm{lp}_\ell(y^P)=\mathbb E_P[u_\ell]\qquad\text{for every user }\ell. \]
Lean: exists_qplan_of_policy isBudgetedPolicy_of_trees · exists_contPlan_of_policy
Q(f) Any monotone, homogeneous objective

Let \(W\) be a function of the users' expected utilities that is

  • monotone: if every user is better off, \(W\) does not go down;
  • positively homogeneous: \(W(\gamma u)=\gamma W(u)\) for \(\gamma\ge0\).

If the plan is \(\varepsilon\)-optimal for \(W\) (no feasible plan beats it by more than \(\varepsilon\)), then against every budgeted policy \(P\):

\[ W\big(\mathbb E[u(\text{Q-SDH})]\big)\ \ge\ \gamma_1\gamma_2\Big(W\big(\mathbb E_P[u]\big)-\varepsilon\Big),\qquad \gamma_1\gamma_2\in\{1/4,\ c_K^2\}. \]

Covered and proved in Lean: max-min over all users or any subset (for example, the users who can match at all), max-min of weighted or normalized utilities, weighted total welfare. Also covered by the hypotheses but not instantiated in Lean: Nash welfare (the geometric mean).

8How the proofs work

8.1 Calls (Q(b))

The query coin of a pair is independent of its values. A query charges \(\ell\) either \(1\), or \(1\) exactly when the other side said yes. Averaging: \(\mathbb E[\text{charge}]=\tau_e\cdot a^\ell_e\). Sum over the user's pairs. In the continuous file this is one integral per pair, moved off the product measure with integral_coord (the expectation of a function of one coordinate is its integral under that coordinate's law).

8.2 Every user keeps a share (Q(c))

Discrete priors. After thinning, pair \(e\) is a candidate in combination \(c\) with probability exactly \(x_{e,c}\), independently across pairs. The max-min work (MaxMinSDH.lean) proved that in this situation a pair's expected value to \(i\) factors as

\[ \mathbb E[\text{gain}]\cdot\kappa_1\cdot\kappa_2 , \]

where \(\kappa_1\) is the chance that stage 1 keeps the pair given it is a candidate and \(\kappa_2\) the chance that stage 2 keeps it given stage 1 passed it on. Balance gives \(\kappa_1\ge\gamma_1\) and \(\kappa_2\ge\gamma_2\). That theorem only needs \(x_{e,c}\le\tau_e\pi_{e,c}\), so it applies to any query-aware plan unchanged.

Continuous priors: the coarsening lemma. The rules never look at values. So, given the candidate pattern \(\eta\) (which pairs are candidates), \(i\)'s utility is

\[ \sum_j w_I(s_{ij})\,\kappa_{ij}(\eta), \]

where \(\kappa_{ij}(\eta)\) is the chance the two stages keep \((i,j)\) given \(\eta\), and \(\kappa_{ij}(\eta)=0\) when \((i,j)\) is not a candidate (keep2, keep2_none). Averaging over values and candidate coins, every term is a product of functions of one pair each. Fubini for such products (integral_fintype_prod_eq_prod from Mathlib) turns the expectation into

\[ \mathbb E\big[w_I(s_{ij})\,\kappa_{ij}(\eta)\big]=\sum_{\eta}\kappa_{ij}(\eta)\prod_{e}\Big(\int f^{\eta}_e\,d\mu_e\Big), \]

where \(f^\eta_{ij}=w_I\,y_{ij}\) if \(\eta\) makes \((i,j)\) a candidate and \(0\) otherwise, and \(f^\eta_e\) is \(y_e\) or \(1-y_e\) for every other pair. Only three numbers per pair appear: \(X_e\), \(G^I_e\), \(G^J_e\). This is integral_prodExp_bern.

The surrogate market. Build a discrete market with one combination per pair that always occurs, worth \(\bar w_I=G^I_e/X_e\) to \(i\) and \(\bar w_J=G^J_e/X_e\) to \(j\), and the plan "use \(e\) with probability \(X_e\)" (surrogate, surPlan). Its candidate law is Bernoulli\((X_e)\) and its expected gains are \(X_e\bar w=G_e\), the same three numbers. So:

  • contUtilI_eq: Q-SDH's expected utility for every user equals MM-SDH's in the surrogate market, for any rules.
  • lpSurplus_surrogate: the planned surpluses are equal too.
  • So the discrete theorems (mmUtilI_ge, mmsdh_quarter, mmsdh_cK) transfer directly.
Two details the reduction needed
  • Surrogate pairs must be acceptable, or the rules would see different candidates. That needs \(G_e\gt0\) whenever \(X_e\gt0\). It holds because \(y_e=0\) off the acceptable set, where both net values are positive (gainP_pos, via "positive integral iff positive-measure support").
  • Pairs that are never used (\(X_e=0\)) get the placeholder value \(1\). That is harmless because \(G_e=0\) then too (gainP_eq_zero), so \(X_e\bar w_e=G_e\) still holds (candP_mul_wbar).

8.3 Total welfare with SDH's own selection (Q(d))

In every realization, the two rule stages select a set that is capped and made of candidates, and candidates are found pairs. So the best capped set of found pairs weighs at least as much (rules_le_bestMatch). Taking expectations, SDH's welfare is at least the users' total utility under the rule mechanism. With \(c_K\)-balanced rules, Q(c) summed over all users gives \(c_K^2\sum_\ell\mathrm{lp}_\ell(y)\).

To compare the two in the same probability space, each pair gets three outcomes given its values: candidate (probability \(y_e(s)\)), found but not kept (\(\tau_e\mathbf 1[s\in A_e]-y_e(s)\)), not found (the rest). Forgetting the middle distinction gives SDH's "found" law, and merging the last two gives the candidate law (prodExp_map).

Total, not per user

SDH's own selection can starve one user. The max-min work proved a counterexample where the worst-off user gets \(\theta^*/3\) (maxMin_sdh_counterexample). That is why per-user guarantees use the rule-based selection.

8.4 No budgeted policy beats the plan (Q(e))

What a budgeted screened policy is. It can query pairs one at a time, choosing each query using coins and every answer so far. It matches only pairs it has queried and found acceptable, within caps, and its expected calls are within budget. Given all values \(\omega\), write

  • \(q_e(\omega)\): the chance it queries \(e\);
  • \(m_e(\omega)\): the chance it matches \(e\).

Then \(m_e\le q_e\), \(m_e=0\) unless \(e\) is acceptable, and \(\sum_{e\ni\ell}m_e\le K_\ell\). The one property that matters is non-anticipation: \(q_e(\omega)\) does not depend on \(e\)'s own values. The decision to query \(e\) is made before \(e\)'s values are seen, so the path to that decision never looks at them. Lean proves this for every random mixture of adaptive query trees (QTree.mem_run_update, isBudgetedPolicy_of_trees).

The induced plan.

\[ \tau^P_e=\mathbb E[q_e(\omega)],\qquad y^P_e(s)=\mathbb E\big[m_e(\omega\text{ with }\omega_e:=s)\big]. \]

The second is "how often the policy matches \(e\) when \(e\)'s values are \(s\)", with everything else averaged out.

The key tool: resampling. Replacing pair \(e\)'s values by a fresh independent draw from \(\mu_e\) does not change the joint law of all values (measurePreserving_update, proved with Mathlib's Measure.pi_eq). Integrated, for any integrable \(f\):

\[ \mathbb E\big[m_e(\omega)\,f(\omega_e)\big]=\int f(s)\,y^P_e(s)\,d\mu_e(s). \]

This is integral_mul_coord_eq. Each constraint of the plan follows from it:

  • \(f=w_I\): the planned surplus equals the policy's expected utility.
  • \(f=1\): planned matches equal the policy's expected matches, so the caps hold.
  • Non-anticipation: \(q_e(\omega\text{ with }\omega_e:=s)=q_e(\omega)\), so \(y^P_e(s)\le\mathbb E[q_e]=\tau^P_e\).
  • The same identity with \(q\) in place of \(m\) gives the policy's expected calls as \(\tau^P_e a^\ell_e\), so the budget carries over.
Example: an adaptive policy and its plan

From the truthful-swiping note's worked example: Ann values Bo, Cy and Dee so that each pair is acceptable with probability \(1/2\), with net weights \(4\), \(2.5\) and \(2\); Ann's cap is \(1\). Policy: query Bo; if acceptable, match. Otherwise query Cy; if acceptable, match. Otherwise query Dee.

  • Query chances: \(\tau_{\text{Bo}}=1\), \(\tau_{\text{Cy}}=1/2\), \(\tau_{\text{Dee}}=1/4\). Each depends only on the other pairs' answers.
  • Use chances when acceptable: \(y_{\text{Bo}}=1\), \(y_{\text{Cy}}=1/2\), \(y_{\text{Dee}}=1/4\), each equal to its \(\tau\).
  • Value \(\tfrac12\cdot4+\tfrac14\cdot2.5+\tfrac18\cdot2=2.875\); Ann's expected calls \(1.75\); expected matches \(7/8\le1\).

So this adaptive policy is one feasible plan of the Q-LP with Ann's budget \(1.75\), and Q-SDH run on the best such plan gets at least \(c_1^2\approx0.40\) of it without ever adapting.

8.5 Objectives (Q(f))

Three steps, with \(\gamma=\gamma_1\gamma_2\):

  1. By Q(c), \(\mathbb E[u_\ell]\ge\gamma\,\mathrm{lp}_\ell(y)\) for every user. Monotone and homogeneous give \(W(\mathbb E[u])\ge W(\gamma\,\mathrm{lp}(y))=\gamma\,W(\mathrm{lp}(y))\).
  2. By Q(e), \(W(\mathbb E_P[u])=W(\mathrm{lp}(y^P))\) for a feasible plan \(y^P\).
  3. \(\varepsilon\)-optimality: \(W(\mathrm{lp}(y^P))\le W(\mathrm{lp}(y))+\varepsilon\).

Max-min is \(W(u)=\min_\ell u_\ell\). A user with no possible partner forces this to \(0\) for every policy, so the theorem also allows the minimum over any nonempty set of users, or \(\min_\ell u_\ell/M_\ell\) with positive weights such as each user's best case (isMonoHom_inf', isMonoHom_weightedInf).

10Assumptions and modeling choices

Each item says what was assumed, why the proof needs it, and what changes without it.

1. Pairs are independent
The values of different pairs are drawn independently. Within a pair, \(q_{ij}\) and \(q_{ji}\) may have any joint law. WhyBoth the resampling step (Q(e)) and the coarsening and contention-resolution steps (Q(c), Q(d)) factor expectations pair by pair. Without itCorrelated priors (for example, a user who is popular with everyone in a way the prior doesn't capture) break both the benchmark bound and the constants.
2. Priors are probability laws with finite means
\(\mathbb E|q_{ij}|\) and \(\mathbb E|q_{ji}|\) are finite (Regular). Continuous, discrete and mixed priors are all allowed. WhyExpected utilities are integrals of net values; they must exist. Without itHeavy-tailed values with no mean make expected utility undefined.
3. A truthful, accurate oracle; known costs and caps
Inherited from the truthful-swiping note: a query reveals a value exactly, and costs and caps are known to the platform. WhyScreening relies on the answers being right. Without itStrategic or noisy oracles are open; the truthful-swiping note shows SDH is manipulable through reports.
4. Utility is additive within the cap
A user's utility is the sum of its net values over its matches. This covers additive users and top-\(k\) users whose cap is at most \(k\). WhyPlanned surplus and expected utility are then sums over pairs. Without itGeneral subadditive users are handled only through cap 1 in the earlier notes.
5. A fixed asking order inside a pair
Both Q-SDH and the benchmark ask the side less likely to accept first. WhyIt fixes how calls are charged to each user (\(a^\ell_e\)). Without itA benchmark that chooses the order pair by pair needs one \(\tau\) per order; the proof should go through the same way but isn't done.
6. Budgets bound expected calls
Under the prior, not in every realization. WhyThe plan's constraints are linear in \(\tau\) and the queries stay independent. Without itA hard cap needs dependent rounding or truncation, which breaks the independence the constants rely on. On the real ratings the overshoot reached 9.8 calls at a budget of 8.
7. The benchmark is a class of screened, non-anticipating policies
Policies match only pairs they queried and found acceptable. Lean describes them by the law of their (queried, matched) sets given all values (discrete), or by the marginals \(q_e,m_e\) (continuous), with caps holding in expectation given the values. WhyThat is everything the proof uses. The class contains every adaptive policy, so the bound is, if anything, stronger than needed. Not coveredPolicies that show unscreened profiles and learn from swipes. For top-\(k\) users those can't be truthful cheaply (Proposition 6 of the truthful-swiping note).
8. Selection rules see only the candidate sets
Rules choose from which pairs are candidates, with their own coins, never from values or swipes. WhyIt keeps the mechanism swipe-local (so truthful), makes users' selections independent (needed for \(\kappa_1\kappa_2\)), and is what makes the continuous case collapse to three numbers per pair. Trade-offSDH's max-weight selection uses values; it gets total welfare (Q(d)) but not per-user guarantees.
9. Thinning is part of the per-user mechanism
Q-SDH keeps a found pair with probability \(y_e(s)/\tau_e\) before selecting. WhyIt makes candidates appear with exactly the plan's probabilities, which balanced rules need. Trade-offIt throws away found pairs below the plan's bar. Filling spare slots afterwards with found pairs never hurts anyone under additive utility; that variant ("random + fill") was tested on data but is not in Lean.
10. Objectives compare expected utilities
\(W\) is applied to the vector of expected utilities (ex ante): max-min means "maximize the smallest expected utility". WhyExpected utilities are linear in the plan, so Q(e) gives an exact match. Not covered\(\mathbb E[W(u)]\), such as the expected minimum, is a different and harder objective.
11. An \(\varepsilon\)-optimal plan, not an exact optimum
WhyWith continuous priors the plan is infinite-dimensional and an exact optimum need not exist. An \(\varepsilon\)-optimal plan always exists. Outside LeanHow to compute the plan (for example on quantile slices of each prior). Every guarantee is relative to the plan actually run.
12. The \(c_K\) rules exist but aren't explicit
WhyTheir existence comes from a minimax theorem. Trade-offRandom order is explicit and gets \(1/4\) instead of \(c_K^2\) (about \(0.53\) at \(K=2\)).
13. How randomness is modeled in the continuous file
Values use Mathlib's product measure. The mechanism's coins, given the values, are finite sums: a pair is a candidate with probability \(y_e(s)\). The query coin enters only the calls, as \(\tau_e\cdot\mathbb E[\text{charge}]\). SDH's value uses "found" with probability \(\tau_e\mathbf 1[s\in A_e]\). WhyThat is exactly the joint law of values and coins, written without building extra measure spaces.
14. Measurability
Plans \(y_e\) and policy marginals \(q_e,m_e\) are measurable functions. A technical requirement for integrals to make sense; every plan or policy one would write down satisfies it.

12Not covered

  • Hard per-user caps on calls instead of a cap on expected calls.
  • Better constants. \(1/4\) and \(c_K^2\) are what the analysis gives; on the speed-dating data users got 0.63–0.92 of their planned surplus.
  • Computing the continuous plan, including its perspective form \(\tau_eF_e(x_e/\tau_e)\).
  • Leximin, which is not a real-valued objective. Q(c) still holds user by user.
  • Nash welfare and \(p\)-means: covered by Q(f)'s hypotheses, not instantiated in Lean.
  • A benchmark that chooses the asking order pair by pair.
  • Strategic or noisy oracles.
